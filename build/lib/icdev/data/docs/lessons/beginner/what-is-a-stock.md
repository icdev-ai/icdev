# What is a stock, really?

A **stock** is a small slice of ownership in a real company.

When you buy 1 share of Apple (AAPL), you own one very-tiny-piece of Apple
Inc. If Apple has ~15 billion shares outstanding, your 1 share is
1 / 15,000,000,000 of the whole pie. That's it. No secret algorithms,
no special maneuver — just fractional ownership.

## What you're actually owning

When you own shares you get:

1. **A claim on future profits** — if Apple keeps selling iPhones, shareholders
   share in the growth. Sometimes that comes as dividends (cash paid to owners);
   more often it's reflected in a rising share price as the company becomes more valuable.

2. **A voting right** — usually tiny. Unless you own a huge chunk, your vote
   on board elections doesn't sway much. Still exists, though.

3. **A residual claim if the company is sold or liquidated** — after debts are
   paid, whatever's left goes to shareholders. In practice, by the time this
   matters, it's usually too late.

## Why share prices move

A stock's price is basically the market's current guess at what all those
future profits (and the company's assets, and its market position) are worth
today. When the market gets more optimistic — AAPL announces record iPhone
sales, or interest rates drop so future cash flows are worth more now —
the price goes up. When pessimism sets in, the price drops.

**This is why patience helps:** over a few days, prices move on sentiment
and noise. Over years, they roughly track actual company earnings.

## Market cap — the number that tells you how big a company is

If Apple has 15B shares outstanding and each share costs $200, Apple's
**market capitalization** (market cap) is 15B × $200 = $3 trillion.

Market cap tells you how big the company is, not how "expensive" the stock is.
A $200 stock isn't "more expensive" than a $20 stock — it depends on how many
shares exist and what the company earns per share.

## What does this mean for you as an investor?

- **You're buying future cash flows at a price.** If you pay $200 for a share
  that earns $8/year, your earnings yield is 4%. Compare that to bonds, CDs,
  and other stocks.

- **You're taking on the company's risks.** If Apple stumbles — product fails,
  competitor wins, tax policy changes — you bear the loss along with other
  owners. That's why you diversify (see lesson 3).

- **You're partnering with management.** Good leadership compounds; bad
  leadership destroys value. This is why who runs the company matters.

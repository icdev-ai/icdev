Bias Color Rating Scale

Each article is scored on a 1-5 scale measuring how biased it is — not whether it leans left or right. Higher scores mean more balanced, transparent reporting. Based on FAR 15.305 evaluation ratings.

BLUE 4.5-5.0
Exceptional
Multiple viewpoints presented fairly. Claims sourced with evidence. No loaded language. Reader can form their own conclusion.
PURPLE 3.8-4.4
Outstanding
Mostly balanced with minor blind spots. Good source diversity. Occasional framing choices but overall fair.
GREEN 3.0-3.7
Acceptable
Reasonably fair but some perspectives missing. Standard news coverage without deep analysis. Wire service articles often land here.
YELLOW 2.0-2.9
Marginal
Significant bias detected. Loaded language, one-sided framing, or statistics without context. Read critically.
RED 0.0-1.9
Unacceptable
Heavily one-sided, misleading, or manipulative. Multiple bias indicators present. Treat with extreme caution.

The 5 Bias Dimensions

Every article is scored across 5 independent dimensions. The composite score is the average. Click "Bias Detail" on any article to see the dimensional breakdown.

1. Loaded Language

Detects emotionally charged or manipulative words: radical, extreme, dangerous, shocking, devastating, catastrophic, propaganda, corrupt.

High score: "The government announced a new policy" — Low score: "The radical regime unleashed a dangerous plan"

2. Framing Balance

Does the article present multiple viewpoints? Looks for balancing phrases: "however", "on the other hand", "critics argue", "supporters say", "in contrast".

High score: "Supporters praised the move; however, critics warned of unintended consequences" — Low score: Single-viewpoint reporting with no counterargument

3. Statistical Honesty

Are statistics presented with context? Checks for base rates, comparisons, sample sizes, and margins of error.

High score: "Crime rose 15% compared to last year's historic low" — Low score: "Crime rose 500%!" (without mentioning it went from 2 to 12 incidents)

4. Source Transparency

Are sources named and diverse? Named experts and official sources score high. Anonymous "sources say" or "insiders claim" score low.

High score: "According to Dr. Smith at MIT and a Pentagon spokesperson..." — Low score: "Sources say... insiders claim... reportedly..."

5. Omission Severity

Are key counterpoints being omitted? Detects assumptive language that shuts down alternative views: "everyone knows", "obviously", "clearly", "no one disputes", "the truth is".

High score: Acknowledges complexity and alternative interpretations — Low score: "Everyone knows this is the only solution. The truth is obvious."

Source Credibility Grades

Each source is rated for credibility (A–F) and political leaning based on data from Media Bias/Fact Check, AllSides, and Ad Fontes Media. INTaaS currently rates 77 outlets. Sources not in the database show "Not Rated."

A
Highly Credible
Established outlet with strong editorial standards and fact-checking. Examples: AP, Reuters, BBC, NPR, The Economist.
B
Generally Credible
Reliable with occasional bias in framing or topic selection. Examples: CNN, NY Post, National Review, Al Jazeera.
C
Mixed Credibility
Some factual reporting but notable bias or misleading framing. Examples: Fox News, Daily Caller, Consortium News.
D
Low Credibility
Significant bias, propaganda elements, or state-affiliated. Examples: RIA Novosti, Xinhua, Global Times, Breitbart.
F
Unreliable
Propaganda outlet, frequent misinformation, or state-controlled media. Examples: RT (Russia Today), Press TV.
N/R
Not Rated
This source is not yet in our credibility database. Bias scoring still applies based on article content analysis.

Political Spectrum

Political leaning shown alongside the credibility grade (e.g., "A center", "B left-center"). This is the outlet's overall editorial tendency, not the bias of any individual article. INTaaS uses a 7-point scale:

Far Left Left Left-Center Center Right-Center Right Far Right

Important: Political leaning is the outlet's tendency, not a judgment of quality. A "left" or "right" outlet can still produce high-quality factual journalism. The credibility grade (A–F) measures quality; the political label measures tendency.

How to Read Your Results

Same story, 20 sources, all GREEN (3.0-3.6):
Wire service articles (AP, Reuters). Factual but thin — no deep analysis or diverse viewpoints. The baseline.

Mixed YELLOW + GREEN:
Some sources are framing the story with bias (loaded language, one-sided framing), others are more neutral. Compare YELLOW articles against GREEN ones to see how the framing differs.

One RED outlier among GREEN sources:
That source is presenting a dramatically different narrative. Worth asking: "Why does their coverage differ so much? Are they seeing something others miss, or are they being manipulative?"

Coverage Gaps:
Facts mentioned by only a few sources out of many. Could be a deliberate omission (suppression) or simply not relevant. The Socratic dialogue helps you think through which it is.

Scoring Methodology

Layer 1: Deterministic (keyword analysis)
Pattern-matching for loaded words, balancing phrases, statistical context, source attribution, and assumptive language. Fast, reproducible, transparent — every score can be traced to specific words in the text.

Layer 2: LLM-as-a-Judge (Claude Haiku)
AI evaluator adds semantic understanding: detecting cherry-picked evidence, correlation-as-causation fallacies, sensationalized headlines vs. actual content, and deeper framing analysis that keyword matching cannot catch. Used for Socratic dialogue, contrarian view generation, and article translation.

Layer 3: Source credibility (77 outlets rated)
Per-outlet credibility grade (A–F) and political leaning from MediaBias/FactCheck, AllSides, and Ad Fontes Media. Provides historical context for each source regardless of individual article scores.

Data source: GDELT
Articles sourced from the Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone — free, 65 languages, updated every 15 minutes, covering global media since 1979. Non-English articles are auto-translated to English.

# AI Workforce Strategy — Upskilling vs. Hiring vs. Attrition

"Will AI replace my team?" is the wrong question. The right question is: "Which roles will AI transform, and is my team positioned to lead that transformation or be left behind by it?"

## AI Role Taxonomy — Three Categories

| Category | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **AI-Aware** | Understands AI capabilities and limitations; uses AI tools provided by others | Program managers, contracting officers, analysts using AI-assisted dashboards |
| **AI-Capable** | Configures, prompts, and operationalizes AI tools; builds non-code AI workflows | Intelligence analysts, budget analysts, HR staff using AI platforms |
| **AI-Native** | Designs and builds AI systems; works in AI infrastructure and models | Data scientists, ML engineers, AI architects |

Most of your workforce needs to reach AI-Aware or AI-Capable. Very few roles require AI-Native.

## DoD AI Workforce Framework

The DoD has published a tiered AI workforce development framework that maps roles to required competencies. It distinguishes between AI users (all staff), AI practitioners (power users and operators), and AI developers (builders). This maps directly to the taxonomy above.

## Build vs. Buy Talent

- **Upskilling** — Your existing staff know the mission. Training them on AI tools is often faster and more durable than hiring outside talent that lacks mission context.
- **Hiring** — AI-Native roles (ML engineers, data scientists) are scarce and expensive. The market is competitive and clearance timelines are long.
- **Reskilling ROI** — A mid-career analyst retrained on AI-assisted tools costs roughly 20% of the fully-loaded hiring cost for an equivalent external hire, and is available immediately.

## The "Will AI Replace My Team?" Answer

AI does not eliminate mission knowledge, stakeholder relationships, or contextual judgment. It eliminates repetitive, rule-governed tasks. Your team's value shifts — it does not disappear.
